Milestone M1 13.10.25 • Fiki & Project Description

BeaconX Fiki

Welcome to our Fiki (Fancy Wiki). This is where we document our journey, our progress, and our validation. We chose a Fiki because standard wikis are boring, and we want this to look as good as the product we're building.

The Problem

We're builders. We attend crypto events, hackathons, and we build protocols. And we noticed something annoying: reliable access to on-chain events is expensive and complex. You either pay a fortune for enterprise RPCs just to listen to a few events, or you waste weeks maintaining your own observer infrastructure. It's a distraction from building the actual product.

Our Solution

BeaconX. We run the heavy infrastructure—the observers, the queues, the redundancy. You just rent a queue. Access is gated by an NFT. You hold the NFT, you get the stream. It's that simple. Automated, reliable, and way cheaper than running your own nodes.

Who is this for?

Blockchain developers, DAO facilitators, and protocol teams. Anyone who needs to know "what just happened on-chain" without the headache of managing the infrastructure to find out.

The Competition

Right now? You either roll your own stack (painful) or overpay for generic RPC providers (expensive). There's no direct competitor offering this specific "NFT-gated event queue" model.

Our Edge

Focus. We do one thing: event streaming. And we use the tech we're building on—NFTs for access control. It's crypto-native infrastructure for crypto-native teams.

Cost Structure

  • Infrastructure: ~$200/mo for DigitalOcean droplets (Primary + Standby).
  • Scalability: Elastic bandwidth/storage that grows with usage.

Revenue Streams

  • Subscriptions: Monthly fee per active queue (paid in crypto/stablecoins).
  • Secondary Market: Potential royalties from NFT access trading (future).

Milestone M2 Validation 1 · 27.10.25

Validation 1: The Idea

We didn't just guess that people wanted this. We went out and asked them. This milestone documents our initial customer discovery process.

How we did it

We didn't hire a research firm. We just went to where our potential users hang out: Telegram groups, Discord servers, and hackathons. We talked to 8 different teams—from solo devs to infrastructure leads at major protocols.

The Question

"How much time and money do you waste maintaining observer nodes just to listen to a few events?"

The Reaction

"Too much. Please build something that solves this."

What we learned

Finding #1: Everyone hates running nodes

Every single developer we spoke to confirmed that maintaining high-availability infrastructure is a pain. It's expensive, it breaks, and it's boring.

Finding #2: They love the NFT idea

Using an NFT for access control clicked immediately. It means they can rotate keys easily, trade the access if they don't need it, and it fits their existing crypto-native workflows.

Finding #3: Replay is a killer feature

Devs told us that handling client disconnections is a nightmare. Our built-in message replay buffer was the feature that got them most excited.

Proof of Validation

Real conversations with real builders.

Verdict: Go!

We have a real problem, a validated solution, and customers waiting for us to launch. We are moving to the next stage: building the MVP.

Milestone M3 12.11.25

Wireframe and Landing Page

Wireframe and landing page documentation for M3, focused only on the requested deliverables.

Wireframe

Wireframe block highlights the entire data path: Observer squads on Server 1 and Server 2 (nodes 0–3) feed Notifier services which hand events into a shared Redis dedupe cache before publication.

  • Observer Layer: Each server runs Observer squads 01/02 with four nodes gathering events and handing them to the local Notifier.
  • Deduplication: Both notifiers write to the shared Redis instance, stripping duplicate payloads prior to queueing.
  • Messaging: RabbitMQ cluster (master + two followers) absorbs the cleaned stream for durable, redundant fan‑out; notifiers publish directly here.
  • Ingress: Nginx gateways sit on top of RabbitMQ consumers, pushing normalized messages toward the load balancer.
  • Load Balancer & App: LoadBalancer spreads traffic across the BeaconX dapp frontends, which surface real-time feeds to users.
  • User Layer: Multiple user cohorts connect through the dapp UI, closing the Observer → Notifier → Redis → RabbitMQ → Nginx → LB → Dapp chain.

This architecture yields high availability, deduped event delivery, and scalable ingress all the way to the BeaconX user experience.

Between 15–17 November we ran a short UX sprint with existing BeaconX operators in Bucharest and Cluj. This section stores the interview transcripts, persona snapshot, story mapping, and the wireframe tweaks we committed to shipping before the next milestone.

Research Objective

Verify whether queue management, alerting, and first-run onboarding match the realities of small validator teams running BeaconX in production.

Participants

Three face-to-face interviews conducted Nov 15–17 with a protocol engineer, a DevOps lead, and a DAO operator who already rely on BeaconX queues.

Outputs

Cleaned transcripts embedded below, a refreshed persona, revised user stories/flows, and annotated wireframes that carry the actual requests.

Interviews took place at TechHub Bucharest (Irina + Laura) and ClujHUB (Mihai).   • Alert routing when Telegram, Slack, and PagerDuty notifications collide.

Interview Synthesis & Conclusions

Across participants we saw the same need for fast context-sharing and low-friction access management. These are the highlights documented for the BeaconX steering log.

  • 01 Incident-first navigation: Anything related to replay/drain/assign must sit on the alert tile itself. Both engineers measured their “acceptable click budget” at two interactions.
  • 02 Delegated NFT use: Teams accept NFT access only if temporary wallets or time-boxed links inherit the same audit log. This shaped the new “share read-only link” UX below.
  • 03 Contextual storytelling: DAO ops need a short narrative (impact, owner, ETA) tied to each queue because they rarely understand RabbitMQ metrics.
  • 04 Guided onboarding: On first login we must auto-suggest chains based on wallet history, pull sample alerts, and spell out what the NFT entitlement covers.

User Stories, Use Cases & Flows

Stories & Use Cases

  1. As a reliability lead, after redeeming an access NFT I want BeaconX to auto-provision observer endpoints and send a sample event so I know I’m live without filing a support ticket.
  2. As a DevOps engineer I want manual replay, drain, and acknowledgement controls embedded on every alert so I can resolve issues within 60 seconds without switching screens.
  3. As a DAO operator I want a read-only incident timeline link that expires automatically so I can brief stakeholders without granting them full workspace access.
  4. As an infrastructure manager I want to issue 24-hour delegate credentials scoped to my NFT so auditors or contractors can view queues without touching production keys.

Use Cases

  • First-run onboarding for a new rollup launch partner.
  • Incident response while traveling to an Ethereum conference.

Flow A — Redeem & Configure

Milestone M3 10.11.25 · The Build

Validation 2: The Prototype

No more slides. We needed to prove this actually works. We spent two weeks hacking together a prototype that could handle real mainnet traffic without falling over.

The Stack

We kept it boring. Boring is good when you're dealing with money and data.

  • 1. The Nodes: Just standard Go nodes. We run them in pairs because they crash.
  • 2. The Pipes: RabbitMQ. It's old, it's heavy, but it doesn't lose messages.
  • 3. The Gate: A simple API that checks if you hold the NFT. If yes, you get the stream.

The Interface

We built a dashboard. It's not winning any design awards yet, but it lets you spin up a queue and see your data flowing. That's all that matters right now.

Public Launch

We pushed beaconx.app live. It's the bare minimum to explain what we do and get people to the docs.

Milestone M4 24.11.25 · The Reality Check

Validation 3: The Refinement

We showed the prototype to a few infrastructure engineers. They didn't care about the UI. They cared about what happens when things break.

The "Alex" Test

We tested with "Alex" (a real reliability engineer). He didn't ask about pricing. He asked: "What happens when I lose my keys?" and "How do I prove it's your fault when the stream dies?"

He was right. We were missing the safety features that make this usable in production.

The Fixes

1. Emergency Key Rotation

Alex pointed out that if a wallet is compromised, the stream is dead. We added a "Panic Button" to revoke keys instantly without moving the NFT.

2. The "Blame" Logs

Engineers need to know who to blame. We added a detailed connection log so they can see exactly when *their* client disconnected vs. when *our* server dropped them.

3. Visual Auth Path

It wasn't clear if the NFT was actually doing anything. We added a visual indicator showing the permission propagating from Chain → Redis → RabbitMQ. It's satisfying to watch.

Ready?

Alex is happy(ish). That means we're ready for real users.
